After Wagner's enormous, overwhelming sound world, French composers asked:
what if music was about *atmosphere* instead of drama?

## Key Ideas

- **Impressionism** began as a movement in French visual art (Monet, Renoir, Cézanne) in the late 19th century — capturing a *mood* or *impression* rather than a precise picture.
- In music, Impressionism (early 20th century) means:
  - **Tone color (timbre)** is the main expressive tool
  - Dissonant chords don't *resolve* — they float, used for color rather than tension
  - Loose, simple, or through-composed structures (no strict Sonata-Allegro)
  - Mood and atmosphere over narrative or drama
- **Claude Debussy** (1862–1918): studied at Paris Conservatory. Saw his music as a conscious reaction *against* Wagner and German tradition.
- **Maurice Ravel** (1875–1937): also from Paris Conservatory. More melodic than Debussy, but the same focus on orchestration as the primary expressive tool. One of the first composers to use saxophone in an orchestral piece. Also an exceptional arranger — his orchestration of Mussorgsky's *Pictures at an Exhibition* is the definitive version.

<div class="info-box">
  <strong>The Boléro challenge:</strong> Impressionism usually avoids strong melody. So how is Boléro — which is almost <em>nothing but</em> melody — impressionist? Listen for the answer: the entire interest is in the ever-changing orchestration and growing dynamics. That <em>is</em> Impressionism — color over structure.
</div>
