Music is art — but it's also physics.
Today we look at what sound actually *is*, where musical pitches come from, and how instruments work mathematically.

## Key Ideas

### What is sound?
Sound is the **fluctuation of air pressure** — molecules of air being pushed and pulled in waves. If you graphed the air pressure at a single point in space while a note played, you'd see a smooth, repeating wave. Your ears detect these waves through tiny hairs deep inside, which vibrate in response.

### Frequency (Hertz)
- **Hertz (Hz)** = cycles per second — how many complete wave cycles pass your ear every second
- Higher Hz = higher pitch. Lower Hz = lower pitch.
- Concert A = **440 Hz** (440 complete waves per second — 26,400 per minute)

### Overtones (the harmonic series)
Every note you play contains not just one pitch, but a whole family of pitches layered above it — called **overtones** or **partials**.

If the fundamental (lowest pitch) is **110 Hz** (A2):

| Partial | Hz | Pitch |
|---------|-----|-------|
| 1st (fundamental) | 110 | A2 |
| 2nd | 220 | A3 |
| 3rd | 330 | E4 |
| 4th | 440 | A4 |
| 5th | 550 | C♯5 |
| 6th | 660 | E5 |

The overtone series is why instruments have **tone color (timbre)** — different instruments emphasize different partials, making them sound distinctive even on the same pitch.

### How long is an instrument?
The relationship between **frequency** and **wavelength**:

> **wavelength = speed of sound ÷ frequency**
> Speed of sound = 343 m/s

Example: A trumpet's lowest open note is B♭3 (≈ 233 Hz):
> 343 ÷ 233 = **1.47 meters** (about 4'10") — that's the length of a trumpet's tubing uncoiled.

### The Doppler Effect
When a sound source *moves toward you*, its waves get compressed — you hear a **higher pitch**.
When it moves *away*, the waves stretch — you hear a **lower pitch**.

Example: Danny runs at 3 m/s while playing A440:
> Change in frequency = (3 ÷ 343) × 440 = **≈ 3.85 Hz**
> — almost a quarter-step sharp as he approaches, flat as he leaves.

## Why does this matter for musicians?

- **Overtones** explain why two clarinets playing the same note still sound different — they're emphasizing different partials.
- **Intonation** is partly a physics problem: two notes at slightly different frequencies create a **beat frequency** — a wobble you can hear and count.
- **Instrument design** is physics in action: changing the length of tubing (valves, slides, keys) changes the wavelength, which changes the pitch.
