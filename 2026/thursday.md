Some of the world's most familiar tunes have no composer at all — they're *folk songs*,
passed down by ear for generations. Starting in the 1800s, composers began weaving these
melodies into their concert music, and today we follow that thread from Hungary to Bohemia
to England to America.

## Key Ideas

- A **folk song** is the music of a *people*, not a single composer: learned by ear, usually **anonymous**, tied to a community, and **always changing** as it's passed along.
- From the **1800s** on, composers across Europe and America began building concert music out of folk tunes. Each one borrows a tune and then *composes around it* — so listen for what they **add**.
- **Béla Bartók** and **Zoltán Kodály** hiked the Hungarian countryside around 1905 and recorded thousands of village songs on an early phonograph, before modern life erased them — the birth of *ethnomusicology*. Kodály then built a whole way of teaching music (the **Kodály method**) on folk songs — you may have learned to read music this way.
- **Antonín Dvořák** (Czech) urged American composers to build their own music from African-American spirituals and Native American song — and showed how in his "New World" Symphony.
- **Grainger** and **Holst** both collected English folk songs and turned them into cornerstones of the wind-band repertoire — music you might be playing at camp.
- **Aaron Copland** created "the sound of America": wide-open spacing, big clean brass, and borrowed hymn and folk tunes.
- **Polyphony** comes back twice today — Holst stacks *Greensleeves* under the *Dargason*, and Copland turns *Simple Gifts* into a round.

## Listen & Watch

<ul class="recordings">
  <li>
    <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GUvBGZnL9rE" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
      Fisk Jubilee Singers — "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot" (1909 recording)
    </a>
    <span class="rec-note">One of the African-American spirituals that inspired Dvořák. The Fisk Jubilee Singers, from Fisk University in Nashville, toured the world starting in 1871 and brought spirituals to the concert stage for the first time.</span>
  </li>
  <li>
    <a href="https://youtu.be/Q3UqJdGMYhY" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
      Dvořák — Symphony No. 9 "From the New World," Movement I
    </a>
    <span class="rec-note">Written in New York. Dvořák never quotes a real spiritual — he writes brand-new melodies that <em>sound</em> like one, full of the "gapped," pentatonic flavor of the folk music he heard in America.</span>
  </li>
  <li>
    <a href="https://youtu.be/0LrMUi76Vy0" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
      Grainger — <em>Lincolnshire Posy</em>
    </a>
    <span class="rec-note">Grainger took an early phonograph into the English countryside and recorded real village singers. A "posy" is a bouquet of wildflowers — six movements, each a folk song he collected, each trying to capture one singer's personal style.</span>
  </li>
  <li>
    <a href="https://youtu.be/KObrPaWM_dE?t=501" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
      Holst — <em>Second Suite in F</em>, Movement IV: "Fantasia on the Dargason"
    </a>
    <span class="rec-note">The Dargason is an English dance tune from a 1651 dancing manual. Listen for <em>Greensleeves</em> woven in underneath — two folk tunes playing at the same time. Polyphony again!</span>
  </li>
  <li>
    <a href="https://youtu.be/0KxMc_tyQBo" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
      Copland — <em>Fanfare for the Common Man</em>
    </a>
    <span class="rec-note">Written during World War II to honor the ordinary person — not kings or generals. Brass and percussion only, with the wide, open spacing that became "the sound of America."</span>
  </li>
  <li>
    <a href="https://youtu.be/8e3rVcSy3IQ" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
      Copland — <em>Appalachian Spring</em>
    </a>
    <span class="rec-note">A ballet about pioneer newlyweds in Pennsylvania. The finale is a set of variations on the Shaker song "Simple Gifts" — listen for the moment it turns into a round (polyphony one more time).</span>
  </li>
</ul>
